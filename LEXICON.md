# Lexicon

One term per post, in order. Cards in `social/lexicon/`, generated by `scripts/lexicon.py` from `src/animal_spirits_brand/data/lexicon.toml`. Every source names the first statement of the idea or the work that named it; check one before adding an entry.

| # | Term | Definition | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 01 | **animal spirits** *n.* | A spontaneous urge to action rather than inaction. | Keynes, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money, 1936, ch. 12 |
| 02 | **ceteris paribus** *adv.* | Other things being equal. True of every model and of no economy. | Marshall, Principles of Economics, 1890 |
| 03 | **the long run** *n.* | In the long run we are all dead. | Keynes, A Tract on Monetary Reform, 1923, ch. 3 |
| 04 | **paradox of thrift** *n.* | When everyone tries to save more at once, income falls, and total saving may not rise. | Keynes, The General Theory, 1936, ch. 7; named in Samuelson, Economics, 1948 |
| 05 | **beauty contest** *n.* | Choosing not what you think best, but what you think others will think best. | Keynes, The General Theory, 1936, ch. 12 |
| 06 | **liquidity trap** *n.* | When interest rates are near zero, extra money is held, not spent or lent. | Keynes, The General Theory, 1936, ch. 15; the name is D. H. Robertson's |
| 07 | **invisible hand** *n.* | Each seeking his own gain may promote an end that was no part of his intention. | Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776, book IV, ch. 2 |
| 08 | **comparative advantage** *n.* | Both sides gain from trade even when one is better at making everything. | Ricardo, On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation, 1817, ch. 7 |
| 09 | **creative destruction** *n.* | New firms and methods replacing old ones: the essential fact about capitalism. | Schumpeter, Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy, 1942, ch. 7 |
| 10 | **tâtonnement** *n.* | Groping: prices called, revised and called again until every market clears. | Walras, Éléments d'économie politique pure, 1874 |
| 11 | **Pareto efficiency** *n.* | No one can be made better off without making someone worse off. Says nothing about fair. | Pareto, Manuale di economia politica, 1906 |
| 12 | **Say's law** *n.* | Supply creates its own demand. Keynes's summary of it, and his target. | Say, Traité d'économie politique, 1803; Keynes, The General Theory, 1936, ch. 2 |
| 13 | **Gresham's law** *n.* | Bad money drives out good: people spend the debased coin and keep the sound one. | Named by Macleod, Elements of Political Economy, 1858 |
| 14 | **Goodhart's law** *n.* | A regularity used for control tends to collapse once pressure is placed on it. | Goodhart, Problems of Monetary Management: the UK Experience, 1975 |
| 15 | **Lucas critique** *n.* | Relations fitted to past data shift when policy changes, because people respond to the policy. | Lucas, Econometric Policy Evaluation: A Critique, 1976 |
| 16 | **moral hazard** *n.* | Insured against a loss, people take less care to avoid it. | Arrow, Uncertainty and the Welfare Economics of Medical Care, 1963 |
| 17 | **market for lemons** *n.* | When buyers cannot tell good from bad, bad sellers stay and good ones leave. | Akerlof, The Market for 'Lemons', Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1970 |
| 18 | **tragedy of the commons** *n.* | A shared resource is overused because each user bears only part of the cost. | Lloyd, Two Lectures on the Checks to Population, 1833; Hardin, Science, 1968 |
| 19 | **Jevons paradox** *n.* | Using coal more efficiently led to using more coal, not less. | Jevons, The Coal Question, 1865 |
| 20 | **Engel's law** *n.* | As income rises, the share spent on food falls. | Engel, 1857 |
| 21 | **marginal utility** *n.* | What the last unit is worth to you, which is less than the first. | Jevons, Menger, 1871; Walras, 1874 |
| 22 | **Phillips curve** *n.* | Lower unemployment came with faster wage growth, in Britain, 1861 to 1957. | Phillips, Economica, 1958 |
| 23 | **multiplier** *n.* | Spending becomes someone's income, part of which is spent again, and so on. | Kahn, The Relation of Home Investment to Unemployment, Economic Journal, 1931 |
| 24 | **opportunity cost** *n.* | The price of a choice is the best thing you gave up for it. | Green, Pain-Cost and Opportunity-Cost, Quarterly Journal of Economics, 1894 |
| 25 | **rent seeking** *n.* | Spending effort to capture existing wealth instead of making new wealth. | Tullock, 1967; named by Krueger, American Economic Review, 1974 |
| 26 | **Knightian uncertainty** *n.* | Risk has known odds. Uncertainty has none, and that is where profit comes from. | Knight, Risk, Uncertainty and Profit, 1921 |
| 27 | **cost disease** *n.* | A string quartet needs four players, as it did in 1800, and must pay them today's wages. | Baumol and Bowen, Performing Arts: The Economic Dilemma, 1966 |
| 28 | **Malthusian trap** *n.* | Any gain in output is eaten by a larger population, so living standards return to subsistence. | Malthus, An Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798 |
| 29 | **conspicuous consumption** *n.* | Buying goods to be seen buying them. | Veblen, The Theory of the Leisure Class, 1899 |
| 30 | **Dutch disease** *n.* | A resource boom raises the currency and hurts every other export. | The Economist, 26 November 1977 |
